# Coffee and Health in Adults: Benefits, Risks, and Contested Evidence (2015-2026)

**Question:** For healthy adults, what do large cohort studies, meta-analyses and regulator guidance (2015-2026) say about the benefits and risks of moderate coffee consumption (roughly 2-4 cups/day), and where is the evidence contested?   **Depth:** quick preset, 1 round, 20 sources read, 10 central claims verified.

## Summary

Moderate coffee (2-4 cups/day) is linked to lower all-cause mortality and diabetes risk in cohorts and umbrella reviews, largest at 3-4 cups/day [4][20][19] <!-- c015 --> and ~30% lower for high vs low intake [4][18] <!-- c016 -->. EFSA/FDA/ACOG call it safe: 400 mg/day caffeine for adults [1][10][8] <!-- c002 -->, ~200 mg/day in pregnancy [1][5][13] <!-- c003 c007 -->. But Mendelian randomization (MR) finds no genetic/causal link, suggesting confounding [3][9][7][11][4] <!-- c021 c026 -->.

## Mortality and cardiometabolic benefits

Independent cohorts corroborate this: UK Biobank found 12% lower all-cause, 17% lower CV mortality vs none [19] <!-- c015 -->; NIH-AARP found the lowest death hazard at 4-5 cups/day, rising above six [20] <!-- c015 -->. The BMJ umbrella review: 3-4 cups/day gives the largest reduction (all-cause RR 0.83, CV mortality 0.81, CVD 0.85) [4] <!-- c015 -->; higher intake adds no harm, just less benefit [4] <!-- c020 -->. Its diabetes finding is corroborated by an independent 30-study meta-analysis (1.18M participants) [4][18] <!-- c016 -->. A Danish cohort found a U-shaped curve, lowest at medium intake [3] <!-- c022 -->.

## Cholesterol and unfiltered coffee

Coffee's lipid effect depends on preparation: a 14-RCT meta-analysis found a dose-response link to total/LDL cholesterol (p<0.01), replicated independently [6][17] <!-- c011 -->. Unfiltered, not filtered, coffee drives the rise [6][17] <!-- c012 -->; filtered trials showed little increase, hyperlipidemic patients and boiled/caffeinated coffee more [6] <!-- c013 c014 -->.

## Regulatory limits, pregnancy and subgroups

EFSA (archived snapshot) finds single doses to 200 mg and habitual intake to 400 mg/day raise no adult concern, corroborated by FDA [1][10][8] <!-- c001 c002 -->. In pregnancy, EFSA/ACOG cap intake near 200 mg/day (one 12-oz cup), corroborated by an independent review finding no concern up to 300 mg/day [1][5][13] <!-- c003 c007 c008 -->. ACOG warns of sleep disruption, nausea/light-headedness and dehydration [5] <!-- c009 c010 -->. EFSA sets 200 mg/day safe for lactation, insufficient evidence for children [1] <!-- c004 c005 -->; alcohol to 0.65 g/kg doesn't change the single-dose threshold [1] <!-- c006 -->.

## Disagreements and contradictions

The clearest tension is cohorts vs MR. Cohorts and the umbrella review link coffee to lower mortality, CVD and diabetes [4][20][19][18] <!-- c015 c016 -->. A Danish MR study (archived snapshot) found near-null caffeine-allele hazard ratios for IHD, stroke, vascular disease and mortality, despite power to detect a smaller effect [3] <!-- c023 c024 -->; authors conclude confounding likely explains the benefit [3] <!-- c025 -->, echoed by an independent MR study of 15 CV outcomes [9] <!-- c021 -->. A second MR study found high intake linked observationally to higher BMI, blood pressure and lipids (not glucose), with near-null diabetes odds ratios per allele [7] <!-- c028 c029 -->, again suggesting confounding [7][4][11] <!-- c026 -->. The review rates 75% of evidence "very low" by GRADE, calls for RCTs [4] <!-- c018 c017 c019 -->.

## What this report could not find

No source compared decaf to caffeinated coffee, so whether benefits persist without caffeine is unresolved. The dose-response ceiling above ~5-6 cups/day was not tested.
